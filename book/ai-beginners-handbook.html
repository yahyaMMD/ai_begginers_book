Part 1

Understanding AI

Start here. You will learn what artificial intelligence actually is, why it suddenly feels everywhere, and how modern systems produce useful answers from patterns in data.

Chapter 1

What Is Artificial Intelligence?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is software that performs tasks that normally require human intelligence: understanding language, recognizing images, making recommendations, or generating new content.

Definition

Think of AI as a very fast pattern-matching engine. It studies huge amounts of examples, learns statistical relationships, and then applies those patterns to new situations. It does not “think” like a person—but it can produce outputs that look intelligent when used well.

Real-world example

When Netflix suggests a show, YouTube ranks the next video, or Google Maps predicts traffic, an AI model is comparing your behavior to patterns learned from millions of other users.

A short history (the useful version)

  • 1950s–1980s: Rules and logic systems. Computers followed hand-written instructions.
  • 1990s–2010s: Machine learning. Systems learned from data (spam filters, recommendations).
  • 2012+: Deep learning. Neural networks made image and speech recognition practical.
  • 2017+: Transformers. A new architecture unlocked modern language models.
  • 2022–2026: Generative AI went mainstream—ChatGPT, image/video generators, agents, and workplace copilots.

Three types you must know

TypeMeaningExample
Narrow AIGood at one job or a narrow set of jobsSpam filter, face unlock, translation
Generative AICreates new text, images, audio, video, codeChatGPT, Midjourney, Veo, Suno
General AI (AGI)Human-level flexible intelligence across domainsNot achieved yet

Everything you use today is Narrow AI—often generative. AGI is a research goal, not a product you can buy.

Why AI matters now

Three shifts happened at once: models got dramatically better, interfaces became chat-simple, and tools connected to everyday apps (Docs, email, browsers, design tools). That combination turned AI from a lab topic into a daily productivity layer.

Where you already use AI

ProductWhat AI does
Netflix / YouTube / TikTokRanks content likely to keep you watching
Google MapsPredicts routes, traffic, and arrival times
AmazonRecommends products and detects fraud patterns
Banking appsFlags unusual transactions
Healthcare toolsAssists imaging review, triage, documentation

Common myths

Myth → Reality
  • “AI is conscious.” No. It predicts likely next words or pixels.
  • “AI always tells the truth.” No. It can hallucinate confident wrong answers.
  • “AI will replace all jobs overnight.” Unlikely. It changes tasks first; people who use AI replace people who don’t.
  • “You need to code.” No. Chat interfaces are enough to start—and this book assumes zero coding.

The near future (practical view)

Expect more multimodal tools (text + image + voice + video in one chat), more agents that take multi-step actions, and deeper workplace integration. Your advantage is not memorizing every product—it is learning transferable skills: prompting, verification, workflow design, and tool selection.

Key Takeaways

  • AI is pattern recognition and generation at scale—not human consciousness.
  • Today’s tools are Narrow / Generative AI.
  • You already use AI daily; the new skill is directing it deliberately.
  • Always verify important facts; fluency ≠ accuracy.

Action Steps

  1. List five apps you use daily and note where AI might already be helping.
  2. Open ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini and ask: “Explain AI to me like I’m 14, with three everyday examples.”
  3. Create an AI Playbook document for prompts and notes.

Exercise — Spot the AI

Pick one shopping site and one social app. Write three specific AI decisions each product is making for you (recommendations, ranking, moderation, search).

Chapter 2

How Modern AI Works

You do not need a computer science degree. You do need a mental model so AI stops feeling like magic—and so you know why prompts, context, and verification matter.

The five-building-block model

  1. Data: Text, images, audio, code, and other examples the system learns from.
  2. Training: Adjusting millions/billions of internal knobs so predictions get better.
  3. Model: The trained brain that can respond to new inputs.
  4. Inference: Using the model to answer your prompt.
  5. Tools & retrieval: Optional extras—web search, your files, calculators, apps.
Analogy

Training is like studying for years. Inference is like taking an open exam in seconds. Retrieval is like being allowed to open a specific binder of your notes during the exam.

Neural networks & deep learning

A neural network is a stack of simple math units (neurons) arranged in layers. “Deep” learning means many layers. Early layers catch simple patterns; later layers catch complex ones (edges → shapes → objects, or letters → words → ideas).

Transformers (why ChatGPT exists)

Transformers are the architecture behind modern large language models (LLMs). Their key trick is attention: the model learns which words in a passage matter for predicting the next word. That makes long-range context possible (“it” refers to which noun?) and scales extremely well.

Tokens, not words

Models read tokens—chunks of text that may be whole words or pieces (ing, Chat, GPT). Limits are measured in tokens. Rough rule: English ≈ 0.75 words per token (or ~100 tokens ≈ 75 words).

Context windows

The context window is how much text the model can consider at once (your prompt + conversation + uploaded material). Bigger windows help with long documents—but stuffing irrelevant text still hurts quality.

ConceptPlain meaningWhy you care
ParametersInternal learned weightsBigger ≠ always better; quality and training matter
Context windowShort-term working memoryLimits how much you can paste at once
Fine-tuningExtra training for a specialtyMakes a model better at a narrow style/task
RAG / RetrievalFetch relevant docs, then answerGrounds answers in your files or the web
HallucinationConfident wrong outputVerify facts, citations, numbers, quotes

Fine-tuning vs. prompting vs. retrieval

  • Prompting: Guide the base model with instructions and examples. Fastest for beginners.
  • Retrieval: Attach or search documents so answers cite your sources (NotebookLM, Perplexity, enterprise RAG).
  • Fine-tuning: Retrain on specialized data. Powerful but usually overkill for individuals.
Practical implication

Most beginners get 80% of results from better prompts + better source material, not from switching models every week.

Key Takeaways

  • Models predict patterns learned from data; they do not look up a single ‘truth database’ by default.
  • Tokens and context windows set hard limits on what fits in one go.
  • Retrieval and tools reduce hallucinations when accuracy matters.
  • Prompting is your primary control surface.

Action Steps

  1. Ask an AI: “Explain transformers using a kitchen analogy.”
  2. Paste a 1-page article and ask for a summary—then ask which details it might have guessed.
  3. In your AI Playbook, note: Token ≈ word piece; Context = working memory; Verify = non-negotiable.

Chapter 3

Understanding AI Tool Categories

When someone says “I use AI,” ask: which category? Text, search, image, video, audio, coding, productivity, automation, or agents each solve different jobs.

1. Text generation AI

Job: Draft, rewrite, brainstorm, analyze, tutor, plan.

Examples: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek.

Best for: Writing and thinking work. Watch out for: factual errors without sources.

2. Search AI

Job: Answer questions with live web context.

Examples: Perplexity, You.com.

Best for: Current events, product comparisons, quick research with links.

3. Research AI

Job: Go deeper—multi-source synthesis, long-document understanding.

Examples: NotebookLM, Perplexity deep research modes, ChatGPT/Gemini research features.

Best for: Reports, literature-style overviews, studying your own materials.

4. Image generation AI

Examples: Midjourney, Flux, DALL·E, Ideogram, Leonardo.

Best for: Concept art, ads, thumbnails, mockups. Weak at: perfect typography (except specialists like Ideogram), legal/likeness-sensitive work without care.

5. Video generation AI

Examples: Google Veo, OpenAI Sora, Runway, Kling, Pika.

Best for: Short clips, storyboards, social drafts. Still costly/limited for long-form cinema.

6. Audio generation AI

Voice: ElevenLabs, PlayHT. Music: Suno, Udio.

Best for: Voiceovers, podcasts drafts, placeholder scores. Check licensing before commercial use.

7. Coding AI

Examples: Cursor, Windsurf, GitHub Copilot, Replit AI.

Best for: Building software faster. Beginners can use them to learn—but always test code.

8. Productivity AI

Examples: Notion AI, Grammarly, Motion, Fireflies.

Best for: Meeting notes, grammar, scheduling, knowledge bases.

9. Automation AI

Examples: Make, Zapier, n8n.

Best for: Connecting apps: “When X happens, do Y with AI in the middle.”

10. AI agents

Examples: OpenAI agent experiences, AutoGPT-style systems, custom agentic workflows.

Best for: Multi-step goals with tools. Risk: errors compound—start supervised.

Starter stack (most people)

ChatGPT or Claude (thinking/writing) + Perplexity (web research) + NotebookLM (your files) + one image tool. Add automation only after you repeat a task weekly.

Key Takeaways

  • Match the tool category to the job—don’t force ChatGPT to do everything.
  • Search/research tools beat chatbots when citations and freshness matter.
  • Automation pays off on repetition; agents need supervision.
  • A small stack used deeply beats ten shallow subscriptions.

  3. Pick one gap and install only that missing tool this week.

Chapter 4

Large Language Models

A Large Language Model (LLM) is an AI trained on vast text to predict and generate language. Chatbots are interfaces; the LLM is the engine underneath.

What an LLM is good at

  • Drafting and editing text in many tones
  • Brainstorming structures, options, and angles
  • Explaining concepts at different levels
  • Transforming formats (bullets → email → script)
  • Light reasoning and planning (with oversight)

Major model families (2026 landscape)

FamilyMakerStanding strength
GPTOpenAIGeneralist, tools, ecosystem, multimodal chat
ClaudeAnthropicLong context, careful writing, coding/analysis
GeminiGoogleGoogle Workspace + search ecosystem fit
DeepSeekDeepSeekStrong value / reasoning-oriented options
LlamaMetaOpen-weight ecosystem, local/self-host options
MistralMistralEfficient open/European-cloud oriented models

Model names and rankings change quickly. Judge by your tasks: writing quality, tool use, speed, price, privacy, and integrations.

Parameters, memory, reasoning, multimodality

  • Parameters: Capacity of the model. Useful signal, not a scoreboard.
  • Chat memory: Product feature that stores preferences across chats (optional).
  • Reasoning modes: Some products spend more compute to plan before answering—better for hard problems, slower/costlier.
  • Multimodal: Accepts or produces more than text (images, audio, files).
Privacy note

Don’t paste secrets, passwords, unpublished financials, or personal data of others into consumer AI tools unless your plan and policy explicitly allow it.

Choosing and switching LLMs like a pro

Beginners often ask, "Which model is best?" A better question is, "Which model is best for this job, with this data, at this budget?" Treat LLMs like specialists on a bench. One may be stronger at careful writing, another at fast brainstorming, another at coding, another at live web research, and another at multimodal understanding.

NeedModel habit to preferSwitch when...
Polished writingGive voice samples and ask for revisionsThe output sounds generic after two critique passes
AnalysisAsk for assumptions, tradeoffs, and uncertaintyIt ignores constraints or overstates confidence
Long documentsUse large context or Projects-style knowledgeIt forgets earlier sections or invents details
Fresh factsUse search or research tools with citationsAnswers need current prices, dates, laws, or news
Images, files, audioUse multimodal input directlyYou are describing a file that you could simply upload
Tip

Run important prompts in two models, then ask a third pass: "Compare these two answers. Where do they disagree? Which parts are best supported?" This is cheaper than being confidently wrong.

When to switch models

  • After a clear failure: If a model misunderstands the task twice, switch instead of endlessly rewording.
  • When the modality changes: A text model may not be the best choice for screenshots, charts, invoices, or photos.
  • When stakes increase: For legal, medical, financial, or public-facing work, use the model/tool with stronger grounding, citations, and review controls.
  • When speed matters: Use smaller/faster models for extraction, tagging, rewriting, and drafts. Save premium models for judgment-heavy work.

Key Takeaways

  • LLM = language engine; chatbot = product wrapper with tools and UI.
  • Pick models by workflow fit, not hype cycles.
  • Use specialized modes (search, reasoning, long context) when the task needs them.

Chapter 6

Claude Complete Guide

Claude (Anthropic) shines at long documents, careful analysis, thoughtful writing, and structured artifacts you can revise.

Strengths to exploit

  • Long context: Large reports, book chapters, codebases summaries
  • Projects: Persistent instructions + knowledge for a workspace
  • Artifacts: Standalone documents, code, or mini-apps in a dedicated panel
  • Writing quality: Strong editorial sense when guided

Writing workflow

Long-form article
Paste brief + sources→Ask for outline→Approve outline→Draft by section→Edit for voice
Project instructions: You are my editorial partner for practical how-to content. Always: ask clarifying questions if the brief is ambiguous; prefer concrete examples; flag unsupported claims. Audience default: smart beginners.

Research workflow

  1. Upload PDFs or paste source notes.
  2. Ask for a source map: themes, agreements, contradictions.
  3. Request a brief with citations to your uploaded text (quotes + locations).
  4. Generate the deliverable only after the brief is solid.
Artifact use case

Ask Claude to produce a one-page competitive teardown as an artifact, then: “Tighten to 400 words and add a decision table.” Iterate in place instead of re-pasting everything.

When to choose Claude

Choose Claude when the input is long, the tone must feel human, or you need careful restructuring. Choose Search AI when you need live web citations first.

Claude Projects: practical setup

A Claude Project is most useful when it has a stable job. Instead of one giant "work" project, create focused spaces such as Newsletter Editor, Client Proposal Assistant, or Support Policy Helper. Add only the knowledge that should shape answers: voice guide, offer details, example work, policies, FAQs, and current constraints.

  1. Name the outcome: "Draft client-ready proposals" beats "Marketing help."
  2. Add project instructions: Role, audience, tone, forbidden claims, preferred format, and when to ask clarifying questions.
  3. Upload source material: Keep it curated. Remove outdated docs so the assistant does not blend old and new facts.
  4. Save winning prompts: Put your best repeat prompts in the Project notes or your AI Playbook.
Artifacts examples
  • A one-page sales proposal that you can edit in the side panel.
  • A simple landing page mockup in HTML.
  • A comparison table for vendor selection.
  • A checklist, rubric, or SOP your team can reuse.

Eight practical Claude prompts

Using the Project knowledge, draft a client proposal for [client]. Include problem, recommended plan, timeline, assumptions, and open questions. Do not invent pricing.
Turn this messy transcript into a clean SOP with owner, trigger, tools, steps, quality check, and escalation path.
Critique this draft against our voice guide. Return a table: issue, why it matters, suggested rewrite.
Create an Artifact: a one-page executive brief from these notes. Use headings, bullets, and a final recommendation.
Act as a skeptical customer. List the objections this landing page does not answer yet.
Summarize these sources into claims we can safely make, claims needing evidence, and claims to avoid.

Action Steps

  1. Create a Claude Project with a one-paragraph house style.
  2. Upload a 10+ page PDF and ask for a 1-page brief with quote-backed claims.
  3. Produce one artifact (checklist or one-pager) for work this week.

Gemini Complete Guide

Gemini is Google’s model family—most valuable when your work already lives in Gmail, Docs, Drive, and Workspace.

Why Gemini is different for beginners

Quality matters, but distribution matters more for daily habits. If AI appears beside the email you’re already writing, you will use it. Gemini’s edge is ecosystem gravity.

Grounding still required

Workspace AI can misread a thread. For commitments, money, or legal wording, read the final text yourself.

Gemini inside Workspace: everyday workflows

Gemini becomes valuable when it works where your files already live. The trick is to use it for bounded tasks: summarize a thread, draft a Doc section, find patterns across notes, or turn a Drive folder into a briefing. Keep sensitive data rules in mind, especially in shared workspaces.

Gmail workflow: thread to decision

  1. Open the thread and ask for the current decision, blockers, and people mentioned.
  2. Ask Gemini to draft a reply with a specific tone and length.
  3. Check names, dates, attachments, commitments, and quoted facts.
  4. Send only after you personally approve the wording.

Docs workflow: rough notes to polished page

  1. Paste messy notes into a Doc.
  2. Ask for an outline first, not a full draft.
  3. Approve the structure, then ask for one section at a time.
  4. Use comments or suggestions for edits instead of overwriting important text.

Drive workflow: folder to brief

  1. Collect the relevant Docs, Sheets, PDFs, and slides in one folder.
  2. Ask for a source-aware summary: what is known, missing, and contradictory.
  3. Ask for a deliverable such as an FAQ, project brief, or decision memo.
Workspace operating loop
Find source→Ask narrow question→Draft→Verify→Share

Six Gemini prompts

Summarize this Gmail thread into decisions, open questions, promised follow-ups, and risks. Quote the message date for each commitment.
Draft a 150-word reply that is friendly, clear, and action-oriented. Include two options for next steps.
Turn this Doc into an executive summary with: context, recommendation, tradeoffs, and next action.

Action Steps

  1. Enable Gemini features available on your Google account/plan.
  2. Pick one recurring email type and save a reply prompt template.
  3. Run a Docs rewrite on a past report: clearer title, sharper exec summary, shorter paragraphs.
Chapter 8

Perplexity Complete Guide

Perplexity is an answer engine: question in, cited synthesis out. Use it when freshness and sources matter more than creative drafting.

Core habits

  • Ask precise questions (“2025–2026”, “for freelancers in the EU”, “primary sources”).
  • Open citations—don’t trust the summary alone.
  • Use follow-ups to narrow: contradictions, costs, alternatives.
  • Switch to deeper research modes for broader reports when available.
What are the main differences between Zapier, Make, and n8n for a 5-person marketing team in 2026? Constraints: compare pricing model shape (not exact cents), learning curve, and best-fit use cases. Output: table + recommendation. Prefer recent sources and cite them.
Research sandwich

Perplexity for sources → Claude/ChatGPT for structuring the narrative → human for conclusions and voice.

Common mistake

Using Perplexity like a creative writer for brand storytelling. It’s a researcher first. Draft final copy in an LLM with your brand voice.

Deep research workflow

Perplexity is strongest when you treat research as a pipeline, not a single answer. Start broad, narrow the question, inspect sources, then synthesize. Your goal is not to collect links; it is to reach a defensible conclusion with citations you would be comfortable showing a manager or client.

  1. Frame the research question: Include geography, date range, audience, and decision you need to make.
  2. Run a broad scan: Ask for the landscape, major viewpoints, and key terms you should know.
  3. Open the sources: Check publication date, author, original data, and whether the page actually supports the claim.
  4. Ask follow-ups: Request counterarguments, recent changes, and primary sources.
  5. Export a brief: Summary, evidence table, open questions, and recommendation.
Citation hygiene

Prefer primary sources, official docs, peer-reviewed papers, government datasets, company filings, and reputable reporting. Do not cite a page just because it was returned. Click it, scan it, and confirm the cited line supports your statement.

Six research prompts

Research [topic] for a beginner business owner in [country]. Give the current landscape, important terms, top risks, and five sources worth reading.
Find recent data on [market]. Prefer primary sources. Return a table with claim, source, date, and confidence.
What are the strongest arguments for and against [decision]? Include citations for each side and note what evidence is missing.
Create a buyer's guide for [software category]. Compare pricing model, best-fit customer, limitations, and recent changes.
Build a timeline of major developments in [topic] over the last 24 months. Cite each milestone.
I need to brief my team on [issue]. Produce a one-page memo with recommendation, evidence, risks, and next questions.

Action Steps

  1. Run three work questions you usually Google; save the best cited answer.
  2. For one question, click every citation and rate trustworthiness.
  3. Add a ‘Research’ section to your AI Playbook with source-check steps.

Part 4

Prompt Engineering

Prompting is the universal skill. Same model, different prompt—completely different results. This part turns vague chatting into professional direction.

Chapter 10

Prompt Engineering Foundations

A prompt is the full instruction you give an AI: task, context, constraints, and desired format. Clear prompts produce clear work.

Why prompts matter

Models are general. Your prompt narrows the universe of possible answers. Professionals don’t “ask AI”—they brief it like a talented junior contractor.

Components of a strong prompt

  1. Role: who the AI should act as
  2. Goal: what done looks like
  3. Audience: who will read/use the output
  4. Context: facts, product, constraints, examples
  5. Process: steps (outline first, then draft)
  6. Format: table, bullets, email, JSON, script
  7. Quality bar: tone, length, must-include / must-avoid
Weak vs strong

Weak: “Write a business plan.”

Strong: “Act as an SMB consultant. Create a 1-page lean plan for a home organizing service in Austin targeting busy parents. Include problem, offer, pricing hypothesis, 3 channels, 90-day milestones. No fluff. Table for milestones.”

Iteration pattern

Pro iteration
Brief→Outline→Draft→Critique→Tighten→Final

Ask the model to critique its own draft against your checklist. Then apply fixes.

The five components of a strong prompt

A prompt is a brief. Good briefs reduce guessing. Most beginner prompts fail because they include a task but omit context, audience, constraints, source material, and output format.

ComponentWhat it answersExample
RoleWho should the AI act like?Act as a practical small-business CFO.
TaskWhat should it produce?Create a cash-flow checklist.
ContextWhat background matters?Audience: first-time agency owner.
ConstraintsWhat boundaries apply?No jargon; max 12 items; flag risks.
FormatHow should output look?Table with priority, action, and why.

Before and after

Before

"Write a marketing plan for my bakery."

After
Act as a local business marketing strategist. Create a 30-day marketing plan for a neighborhood sourdough bakery in Austin. Goal: increase weekday morning foot traffic. Budget: $500. Audience: office workers within 2 miles. Include daily actions, low-cost partnership ideas, email/social copy themes, and how to measure success. Table format.

The second prompt gives the model something to optimize. It can now make tradeoffs instead of writing generic advice.

Temperature and style intuition

If a tool exposes temperature or creativity settings, think of it as a "variation dial." Low settings are better for extraction, summaries, policy replies, and structured outputs. Higher settings are useful for names, hooks, concepts, and brainstorming. If the tool does not expose temperature, you can still steer style with words such as "literal," "conservative," "playful," "unexpected," or "executive."

Key Takeaways

  • Prompts are briefs, not magic words.
  • Format instructions dramatically improve usability.
  • Iteration beats one giant mega-prompt for complex work.
Chapter 11

Prompt Frameworks

Frameworks are training wheels that become instincts. Memorize a few; remix freely.

RTF — Role, Task, Format

Role: Career coach for career-switchers into UX. Task: Create a 30-day learning plan. Format: Week-by-week table with daily time estimates and deliverables.

CRISPE

Capacity/Role · Request · Insight/Context · Statement of goal · Personality/Tone · Experiment/Evaluate

Capacity: Senior B2B SaaS copywriter. Request: Rewrite this homepage H1/H2. Insight: Users fear complex setup; we offer 2-day onboarding. Goal: Increase demo clicks. Personality: Plain-spoken, confident, specific. Evaluate: Give 3 variants and predict which converts best and why.

APE — Action, Purpose, Expectation

Action: Summarize the transcript. Purpose: Prep me for a 10-minute exec update. Expectation: 5 bullets max, each with a decision needed.

Chain of Thought (ask for steps)

For logic, math, or tradeoffs: “Think step by step. List assumptions. Then conclude.”

Tree of Thoughts

Ask for 2–3 distinct approaches, evaluate each, then pick/merge a winner. Great for strategy and creative direction.

Few-shot prompting

Provide 2–3 examples of ideal input→output, then the new input. Best for consistent tone and structure.

Framework picker

RTF for speed · CRISPE for marketing · Few-shot for voice matching · Tree of Thoughts for strategy · CoT for analysis.

Exercise — Upgrade a prompt

Take a vague prompt you used this month. Rewrite it with RTF and again with CRISPE. Compare outputs side by side.

Think in shots, not scenes

Video AI works best when you ask for one clear shot at a time. A "scene" may include multiple camera angles, actions, and timing changes. A "shot" is simpler: one subject, one action, one camera move, one mood. If you need a 30-second ad, plan it as five or six short clips and edit them together.

AI Automation

Chapter 22

Automation Fundamentals

Every automation is: Trigger → (optional filters) → Actions. AI actions transform messy text into structured fields.

  • Trigger: new email, form submit, schedule, webhook
  • Action: create task, send Slack, draft reply, update Sheet
  • API: how apps talk; no-code tools hide most of this
Automation fitness test

Automate if you do it ≥3×/week, rules are clear, and mistakes are reversible or reviewed.

Guardrails

Start with drafts + human approval for anything external (customer email, social posts, payments).

Triggers, actions, and AI in the middle

An automation begins when a trigger happens: a form is submitted, an email is labeled, a row is added, a date arrives, or a webhook receives data. Actions do the work: create a task, send a message, update a CRM, draft an email, or write a file. AI is most useful in the middle, where messy human language must become structured decisions.

AI-in-the-middle patternExampleGuardrail
ClassifyTag support email as billing, bug, refund, or urgent.Escalate if confidence is low.
ExtractPull name, company, budget, and deadline from a form.Validate required fields before writing.
DraftCreate a reply, proposal, or social post.Save as draft for approval.
SummarizeTurn a call transcript into CRM notes.Link back to transcript.
RouteSend leads to the right salesperson.Log the reason for routing.

Failure modes to expect

  • Bad trigger data: missing fields, duplicate records, or unexpected file types.
  • Overconfident AI: the model guesses instead of saying it does not know.
  • Silent breakage: an app permission expires and the workflow stops.
  • Runaway volume: a loop creates hundreds of tasks or messages.
  • No owner: everyone assumes the automation is being monitored.

Decision tree

Should you automate?
Repeated weekly?→Rules clear?→Mistakes reversible?→Human approval needed?→Build small test

Part 10

AI Agents

Agents don’t just answer—they plan and use tools toward a goal. Powerful, but they need boundaries.

Chapter 26

Understanding AI Agents

An AI agent is a system that can set subgoals, call tools (browse, code, email, databases), remember state, and iterate until a stop condition.

Architecture (beginner map)

  • Brain: LLM for planning and language
  • Tools: actions it may take
  • Memory: short-term thread + optional long-term store
  • Planner: break goal → steps → revisit
  • Guardrails: permissions, budgets, human approval
Reality check

Unsupervised agents can loop, spend money, or message customers incorrectly. Start read-only or draft-only.

Agent architecture by analogy

Imagine hiring an intern for a specific job. The LLM is the intern's reasoning and communication ability. Tools are what the intern is allowed to use: browser, email, calendar, database, spreadsheet, code runner, or CRM. Memory is the notebook they keep. Planning is the habit of breaking a goal into steps. Guardrails are the manager's rules: budget, permissions, review points, and when to stop.

Agent partBeginner analogyExample
GoalThe assignmentPrepare a weekly competitor brief.
ToolsAllowed appsSearch web, read Drive folder, create Doc.
MemoryWorking notesPrevious competitor list and style preferences.
PlannerTo-do listFind sources, extract claims, compare, draft.
EvaluatorQuality checkAre all claims cited? Is the brief under one page?

Tools, memory, and planning examples

  • Tool example: A support agent can read a policy notebook and draft replies, but cannot issue refunds without approval.
  • Memory example: A personal planning agent remembers your preferred working hours and recurring goals, but not private data it does not need.
  • Planning example: A research agent first lists questions, then searches, then writes a source table, then drafts a memo.
Risks
  • Compounding errors: One bad assumption can affect many later actions.
  • Tool misuse: The agent may update the wrong record or message the wrong person.
  • Runaway loops: Poor stop conditions can waste time, credits, or API calls.
  • Data exposure: Broad permissions create bigger privacy risk.
Chapter 27

Building Agent Workflows

Practical examples

  • Research agent: gather sources → outline → brief (human publishes)
  • Support agent: retrieve policy → draft → escalate if confidence low
  • Marketing agent: pull product facts → generate campaign pack → Slack approval
Business pattern

Goal: “Prepare a weekly competitor brief.” Tools: web fetch, Drive write, email. Stop when a 1-page Doc exists. Human reviews every Friday.

Safe agent loop
Define goal→Limit tools→Require approval gates→Log actions→Review weekly

Five agent workflow specs

1. Marketing campaign assistant

Goal: create a campaign draft pack from approved product facts. Tools: brand docs, product page, calendar, Doc creation. Output: audience, hooks, email draft, social variants, review checklist. Approval: marketer approves before publishing.

2. Support response assistant

Goal: reduce first-response time. Tools: policy notebook, ticket system, macro library. Output: category, urgency, draft reply, cited policy. Escalate: angry customers, refunds, legal threats, safety issues, low confidence.

3. Research briefing agent

Goal: prepare a weekly brief on a defined topic. Tools: web search, saved source list, Docs. Output: source table, key changes, risks, recommendation. Rule: no uncited factual claims.

4. Operations SOP agent

Goal: turn transcripts and notes into standard operating procedures. Tools: transcript folder, SOP template, task manager. Output: SOP draft, checklist, missing questions. Approval: process owner signs off.

5. Personal weekly planning agent

Goal: convert calendar, tasks, and goals into a realistic week plan. Tools: calendar read access, task list, notes. Output: top outcomes, schedule blocks, risks, prep list. Limit: read-only until trusted.

Action Steps

  1. Write an agent spec: goal, tools, data access, success metric, kill switch.
  2. Implement as a Make/n8n flow before full autonomy.
